# Numbers you will be told that are not real

ASO and app-growth content is an SEO battleground, and a lot of the
statistics in it are laundered: a vendor's sample from 2017 becomes "the
industry average", or a figure is attributed to a source that never
published it. What follows was traced back and either could not be found
at the cited source, or was found and is much weaker than its reputation.

Check anything here before you plan around it.

## Fabrications, traced

These were chased to their supposed origin and are not there. Several
concern widgets specifically, which is the topic with the largest gap
between how confidently it is discussed and how little is known.

- **"Widget users have 15%+ higher 7-day retention."** The cited page is
  a product demo containing no figures at all.
- **"52% of users find widgets valuable."** No source.
- **"600% session increase, 40% duration, 35% retention, 60% fewer
  support tickets"**, all four from one agency post, none sourced.
- **"2.7x higher 30-day retention" from widgets.** A misattributed figure
  about cross-channel messaging.
- **"Apps need a 4.5+ rating to be featured."** Apple has never said this
  and it is not among its seven published criteria.
- **"0.3% Spotlight click-through", attributed to Branch.** Both Branch
  posts about Spotlight contain no statistics at all.
- **"47% of saves came via the share sheet"**, attributed to a GitHub
  issue that does not exist.
- **"Prompt-to-review industry average of 3 to 5%."** No primary source.
- **"30% (or 38%) of users update their review after a developer reply."**
  AppFollow's own methodology implies roughly 11% of review *updates* are
  attributable to a reply, which is a different and much smaller claim.
- **"Ratings stabilise after 50 to 100 reviews."** No source.
- **"Google Play rewards 2 to 3% keyword density."** No source, and
  keyword density is not how either store works.

## Real, but far weaker than its reputation

- **"App of the Day gives +1,747% downloads."** One 30-day sample from
  2017, by a vendor selling app analytics, with no named developer
  supplying a number. The same article notes Starbucks got "a handful of
  downloads" from being featured.
- **Retention benchmarks by category.** SEO aggregators cite Productivity
  D1 retention at both 40 to 50% and 17 to 18%, all attributed to one
  AppsFlyer report that could not be located on AppsFlyer's own site.
  Treat published retention benchmarks as unusable and measure your own.
- **Recency weighting of the App Store summary rating.** Apple has never
  said this. It is true of Google Play and appears to have been copied
  across. AppFollow says the App Store formula counts every review
  regardless of age.
- **Retention, crash rate and uninstalls as App Store search ranking
  factors.** Widely asserted, absent from Apple's stated factors and from
  Apple's own 2026 ranker paper. True for Google Play, which does use
  Android Vitals. Not evidenced for Apple.
- **"Live Activities give 23.7% higher 30-day retention."** Correlational
  and self-selecting: the apps that ship Live Activities are delivery,
  rideshare, sports and transit apps, which have structurally higher
  retention anyway. Methodology unpublished. It is also now the only
  number in circulation on the subject, which is how a weak figure
  becomes a fact.
- **Screenshot caption indexing.** Appfigures claims Apple began
  extracting keywords from screenshot captions and calls it the biggest
  change in years, while conceding there is no good way to prove it.
  ConsultMyApp tested 64 phrases across 8 leading apps: 36 did not rank,
  27 were explained by existing metadata, 1 was unexplained. Confirmed
  Apple use of screenshots is **tag generation**, not keyword ranking.

## Cited for things they do not say

- **"Slopes Diaries #19: App Store Review Replies"** is linked constantly
  as evidence that replying to reviews works. It is entirely qualitative.
  The only number in it is "around 700 reviews". No rating change, no
  reply count, no outcome.
- **Focus Friend reaching number one** is cited as proof of organic
  virality. It was a creator partnership: 20,000 downloads in week one
  from a Hank Green community post. A real and repeatable strategy, and
  not the one it is being used to evidence.

## Where credible sources simply disagree

Not errors. Genuine spread, usually mean versus median or category mix.
Quoting either number as "the" number is the mistake.

- **Apple Search Ads cost per tap:** AppTweak global median $0.92,
  SplitMetrics average $2.25, Adapty US median $1.58. A 2.4x spread. Use
  medians, use your own geography, and never quote a global average to a
  small app.
- **Brand campaign conversion rate:** AppTweak 73%, RocketShip HQ 30 to
  50%. Not a rounding difference.
- **Brand-defence cannibalisation.** The circulating "40% incremental, 60%
  cannibalistic" has no published methodology. INCRMNTAL, who measure
  this commercially, decline to give a percentage at all and report that
  one campaign was incremental and another practically redundant.

## Conflicts of interest worth holding in mind

Most good app-growth data comes from vendors, because vendors are the
only people with the aggregate. That does not make it wrong. It does mean
noticing which direction the error would run if there were one.

- **RevenueCat** publishes the best subscription dataset available and
  sells subscription infrastructure. Its finding that lifetime-dominant
  apps monetise at about half the rate of yearly-dominant apps is
  plausible and also exactly what it would want to be true.
- **ASO tool vendors** publish benchmarks and sell ASO tools.
- **Attribution companies** publish incrementality figures and sell
  attribution.

## The useful habit

Before repeating a number: find the primary source, check the sample size
and the year, and check whether the person who published it sells the
thing the number recommends. Most app-growth statistics fail at least one
of those, and a plan built on one you did not check is a plan you cannot
debug when it does not work.
